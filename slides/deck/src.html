Let's start with a classic example. Here's the question: a 1,100 square foot house in Gombak, what's the rent? Given x, what's y? The size is our input, x. The rent is the output we want, y.

To answer it, we need examples. Every dot here is a house we already know the size and the rent for.

Then we pick a model. The simplest one there is: linear regression. Find the straight line that best fits all those dots. That line is the model. And a straight line is just this equation from school: y equals m x plus c. Here, rent equals one times the size, plus 300.

And out comes the price. Put 1,100 into the equation: 1,100 plus 300. A 1,100 square foot house in Gombak, about RM 1,400 a month. And here the input is, well, numbers. Pretty straightforward.

3 · Model
What's inside a model
every line is a parameter, a knob the model learned

So what's actually inside a model? Let's open one up.

It's layers. Numbers come in on the left, from the input we just talked about. Each layer passes numbers on to the next, layer after layer, and the answer comes out on the right. A big model like Llama has 126 of these layers.

And every one of these lines is a knob, which we call a parameter. Training is just adjusting those knobs, again and again, until the output comes out right. So when people say a "7 billion" model, they mean 7 billion knobs.

So, to recap. Whenever you want to use AI for something, think about three things.

First, the input. What form is your data in? Is it images, voice, text, numbers? Remember, it all has to become numbers in the end.

And third, the output. What do you actually need back? Text? An image? Audio? Or, like today, code?

So for today: your input is your paper. The model is one of these frontier chatbots, and it costs you a few sen. And the output is code, which becomes a working animation of your research.
